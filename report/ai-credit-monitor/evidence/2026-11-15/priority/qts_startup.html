    Blackstone's QTS cuts its AI data center debt sale to $300 million

    Bloomberg reported that QTS, Blackstone's data center arm, trimmed a seven-to-15-year private placement from about $800 million to $300 million after investors pushed back on yields. In April, a QTS bond tied to Microsoft drew $12.5 billion in orders.

    Judith Murphy
    Nov 14, 2026 · 8:55 AM · 4 min read · 1.4K reads

    A top-tier data center owner sought a planned private debt sale and walked away with far less because it wouldn't pay up. If you lend to or build for the AI boom, the price of that money is the number to watch.

    QTS, the data center operator owned by Blackstone, is cutting a planned private debt sale from about $800 million to roughly $300 million, a reduction of about 60%, according to Bloomberg's report on Friday, November 13. Bloomberg said the company struggled to draw enough demand and wasn't willing to pay much higher yields to get it.

    Two reasons were given for the weak response. Some investors balked at the price QTS was offering, and others had hit their limits because QTS has already come to the private placement market several times. Traders Union, summarising the Bloomberg report, framed the cut as a sign of tighter private credit conditions.

    The deal was first reported in late October. Bloomberg said QTS was seeking about $800 million in private placement debt, and Pipeline Road's summary of that report put the maturities at seven to 15 years, with the notes issued through a special-purpose entity called QTS Thunder Managing Issuer LLC. Mizuho, Natixis, Societe Generale and Wells Fargo were named as arrangers. The buyers were meant to be the usual private placement crowd: insurers and asset managers who like long, steady paper.

    Instead of pricing quickly, the deal took about two and a half weeks, and the size shrank. Bloomberg's reporting doesn't say the deal failed, and a $300 million raise isn't nothing. But when a borrower cuts by more than half rather than pay a bit more, it's telling you where it thinks the price line sits.

    Citi says AI data center bonds are finally being priced as the project finance deals they actually are

    Citi analysts say the credit spread on a QTS bond tied to a Microsoft facility has widened more than 30 basis points since April while Microsoft's corporate paper held flat, a divergence the bank attributes to non-amortizing debt structure. The finding signals that bond investors are now pricing project-specific risks independently of hyperscaler... - AI data center bonds project finance pricing - how investors are pricing data center project bonds

    A very different spring

    The contrast with earlier this year is what makes the cut worth noticing. In April, Bloomberg reported that a QTS bond sale tied to a Microsoft-linked data center drew $12.5 billion of orders.

    Since then the mood has cooled in places. Citi analysts said in July that the spread on a QTS bond tied to a Microsoft facility had widened by more than 30 basis points since April, while Microsoft's own corporate debt held flat. Citi's explanation was the non-amortizing structure of the project debt, so the market was pricing the deal as project finance rather than as a Microsoft credit. Octus reported this week that roughly $500 million of first-lien data center loans, including $175 million tied to QTS, were being shopped to high-yield investors just days after the US midterm elections.

    Frankly, none of those data points alone is alarming. Put together, they show the same borrower going from oversubscribed to price-sensitive in seven months.

    Who feels it next

    The AI build-out runs on borrowed money in a way the headlines about chip sales tend to hide. Hyperscalers like Microsoft can fund a lot from cash flow, but the owners and developers who build and lease the buildings lean on bond, loan and private placement markets, and so do the smaller neocloud companies renting out GPUs. If a sponsor as well-funded as Blackstone has to take less money or pay more for it, a weaker borrower should expect worse terms, not better ones.

    That's the link to Nvidia, Oracle and the power equipment makers. Their order books depend on data center projects reaching financial close. A project that can't raise its debt gets delayed or shrunk, and the delay shows up first in a supplier's backlog, not in a press release.

    The larger QTS borrowing, including a reported $4.6 billion green bond, is a separate transaction from this $300 million placement.

    Bloomberg's report didn't give the final pricing or say which investors took the reduced deal.

    Obscure neocloud lenders, not Nvidia, are where the AI bubble could burst first

    CoreWeave carries about $50 billion in debt with bonds pricing in a 63% five-year default risk, while Applied Digital's interest bill now nearly matches its entire annual revenue.
