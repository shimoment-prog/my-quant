A $500 Million Pile of Data Center Loans Just Hit the Auction Block
Bloomberg reports bids are due November 19 on a $500 million data center loan portfolio tied to Vantage Data Centers, CyrusOne and a $25 million OpenAI-backed Stargate site in New Mexico.

Someone is quietly trying to get out of the AI infrastructure trade, one loan at a time. The debt up for sale includes a slice backing OpenAI's own Stargate project.
A mystery seller has put roughly $500 million in data center loans up for auction, and the timing tells you almost everything. Bids are due November 19, each one at least $10 million, according to Bloomberg, which first reported the sale. The portfolio includes debt tied to Vantage Data Centers and CyrusOne, along with a $25 million slice backing a New Mexico site that's part of the Stargate project, the joint venture between OpenAI, SoftBank and Oracle.
This isn't a routine refinancing. It's happening days after Democrats swept both chambers in the November 3 midterms, a result that immediately raised doubts in credit markets about how much political cover the AI buildout still has. Bloomberg's report frames the auction as one of the first of its kind to contain data center debt exclusively, which matters because it means buyers can't hide behind a diversified pool. They're pricing this asset class on its own, in the open, right now.
The loan sale didn't happen in isolation. Andrzej Skiba, head of BlueBay US fixed income at RBC Global Asset Management, told Bloomberg that hedge funds have been "massive sellers" of AI-related debt in recent weeks, describing it as a "massive hedge fund capitulation within the space." That's not a hedge fund quietly trimming a position. That's a desk telling a reporter, on the record, that the smart money ran for the exits before everyone else noticed.
What makes this auction more than a one-off is who else is selling. JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, SMBC and MUFG have all been quietly shopping pieces of the AI debt they helped underwrite, Bloomberg reported, a sign that the lenders closest to the money are hedging their own exposure. These are the same banks that spent 2025 and early 2026 racing to finance hyperscaler and neocloud buildouts. Now they're looking for the door, or at least looking for someone else to share it with.

That's a meaningful shift from earlier in the year, when Skiba described a fixed-income market facing what he called an unprecedented wave of AI-related issuance, with roughly $100 billion in AI-linked debt expected over twelve months. Back then the conversation was about appetite. Now it's about exits.
The stock market is moving the same way. The Nasdaq Composite has fallen into correction territory, down more than 10% from the record high it set in early October, as investors dump AI-adjacent stocks across the board. Palantir alone has given up 17% of its value since hitting an all-time high on November 3. Equity investors are repricing the AI trade in real time. This auction is the credit market's version of the same move, just slower and less visible, and more honest too: a loan price is a number someone actually has to pay.
A steep discount on these loans, if bids come in materially below face value, would be the clearest signal yet that duration risk and policy uncertainty have outrun the returns lenders expected when they wrote these deals. Some lenders are already willing to sell pieces of data center debt to non-bank buyers at a discount just to move it off their books, according to Bloomberg's reporting on the broader financing stress.
The $25 million Stargate slice is the detail worth sitting with. OpenAI, SoftBank and Oracle built that New Mexico project as a flagship bet on AI's physical future, the kind of project politicians on both sides have cited as proof the buildout creates real jobs and real infrastructure. Now a piece of its financing is sitting in a bid pile alongside CyrusOne and Vantage paper, waiting to see what the market thinks it's actually worth once the political tailwind turns into a headwind.
Bids are due November 19, and the winning number will show how much the midterm backlash has cost data center lenders.
