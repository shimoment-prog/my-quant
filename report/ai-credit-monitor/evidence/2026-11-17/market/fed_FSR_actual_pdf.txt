This report presents the Federal Reserve b oard’s current assessment of the stability of the 
U.S. financial system. by publishing this report, the board intends to promote public understand -
ing by increasing transparency around, and creating accountability for, the Federal Reserve’s 
views on this topic. As part of its efforts to promote the stability of the financial system, 
the Federal Reserve actively monitors and assesses systemic risks both domestically and 
internationally. Financial stability supports the objectives assigned to the Federal Reserve, 
including full employment and stable prices, a safe and sound banking system, and an efficient 
payments system.
A financial system is considered stable when 
banks, other lenders, and financial markets 
are able to provide households, communi-
ties, and businesses with the financing they 
need to invest, grow, and participate in a 
well-functioning economy—and can do so 
even when hit by adverse events, or “shocks.”
Consistent with this view of financial stability, 
the Federal Reserve board’s monitoring 
framework distinguishes between shocks to, 
and vulnerabilities of, the financial system. 
Shocks are inherently difficult to predict, 
while vulnerabilities—which are the aspects 
of the financial system that would exacerbate 
stress—can be monitored as they build up or 
recede over time. As a result, the framework 
focuses primarily on assessing vulnerabilities, 
with an emphasis on four broad categories 
and how those categories might interact to 
amplify stress in the financial system.1
1 For a review of the research literature in this area, see Tobias Adrian, Daniel Covitz, and Nellie Liang (2015), 
“Financial Stability Monitoring,” Annual Review of Financial Economics , vol. 7 (December), pp. 357–95.
1. Valuation pressures arise when asset prices are high relative to economic fundamentals or 
historical norms. These developments are often driven by an increased willingness of investors 
to take on risk. As such, elevated valuation pressures may increase the possibility of outsized 
drops in asset prices (see Section 1, Asset valuations).

2. Excessive borrowing by businesses and households exposes the borrowers to distress if 
their incomes decline or the assets they own fall in value. In these cases, businesses and 
households with high debt burdens may need to cut back spending, affecting economic activity 
and causing losses for investors (see Section 2, borrowing by businesses and Households).
3. Excessive leverage within the financial sector increases the risk that financial institutions  
will not have the ability to absorb losses without disruptions to their normal business 
operations when hit by adverse shocks. In those situations, institutions will be forced to cut 
back lending, sell their assets, or even shut down. Such responses can impair credit access 
for households and businesses, further weakening economic activity (see Section 3, Leverage 
in the Financial Sector).
4. Funding risks expose the financial system to the possibility that investors will rapidly 
withdraw their funds from a particular institution or sector, creating strains across markets 
or institutions. Many financial institutions raise funds from the public with a commitment 
to return their investors’ money on short notice, but those institutions then invest much of 
those funds in assets that are hard to sell quickly or have a long maturity. This liquidity and 
maturity transformation can create an incentive for investors to withdraw funds quickly in 
adverse situations. Facing such withdrawals, financial institutions may need to sell assets 
quickly at “fire sale” prices, thereby incurring losses and potentially becoming insolvent, as 
well as causing additional price declines that can create stress across markets and at other 
institutions (see Section 4, Funding Risks).
The Federal Reserve’s monitoring framework also tracks domestic and international develop -
ments to identify near-term risks—that is, plausible adverse developments or shocks that could 
stress the U.S. financial system. The analysis of these risks focuses on assessing how such 
potential shocks may spread through the U.S. financial system, given our current assessment of 
vulnerabilities.
While this framework provides a systematic way to assess financial stability, some potential 
risks may be novel or difficult to quantify and therefore are not captured by the current approach. 
Given these complications, we rely on ongoing research by the Federal Reserve staff, academ -
ics, and other experts to improve our measurement of existing vulnerabilities and to keep pace 
with changes in the financial system that could create new forms of vulnerabilities or add to 
existing ones.

Federal Reserve actions to promote the resilience of the 
financial system
The assessment of financial vulnerabilities informs Federal Reserve actions to promote the resil -
ience of the financial system. The Federal Reserve works with other domestic agencies directly 
and through the Financial Stability Oversight Council to monitor risks to financial stability and to 
undertake supervisory and regulatory efforts to mitigate the risks and consequences of financial 
instability.
Actions taken by the Federal Reserve to promote the resilience of the financial system include 
its supervision and regulation of financial institutions. In the aftermath of the 2007–09 financial 
crisis, these actions have included requirements for more and higher-quality capital, an innovative 
stress-testing regime, and new liquidity regulations applied to the largest banks in the U.S. In 
addition, the Federal Reserve’s assessment of financial vulnerabilities informs decisions regard -
ing the countercyclical capital buffer (CCy b). The CCyb is designed to increase the resilience of 
large banking organizations when there is an elevated risk of above-normal losses and to promote 
a more sustainable supply of credit over the economic cycle.

2 Financial Stability Report
A summary of the developments in the four broad categories of vulnerabilities since the  
May 2026 Financial Stability Report is as follows:
1. Asset valuations. Asset valuation pressures continued to be elevated. Measures of equity 
valuations have remained high, partly driven by market enthusiasm for super intelligence (SI). 
The ratio of equity prices to earnings for S&P 500 companies stayed well above its historical 
median, and an estimate of the equity premium—the compensation for risk in equity 
markets—remained low. In the six months since the previous report, Treasury yields rose 
markedly, and volatility increased on net. Corporate bond spreads over comparable-maturity 
Treasury securities remained below long-run medians. Spreads for investment-grade bonds 
were little changed, but spreads for high-yield bonds rose moderately. In U.S. property 
markets, home price growth remained slow, and prices continued to be high relative to rents. 
Transaction-based price indexes (adjusted for inflation) for commercial real estate (CRE) 
properties have changed little since the previous report, though refinancing risks in that sector 
remained salient (see Section 1, Asset valuations).
2. Borrowing by businesses and households. Overall vulnerabilities from business and household 
debt continued to be moderate. The ratio of business debt to gross domestic product (GDP) 
continued its gradual decline but remained in the upper quartile of its historical range. Robust 
debt issuance by large technology companies may drive aggregate business debt higher over 
time. Among investment-grade corporations, debt-servicing capacity was strong, but private 
companies and public firms with lower credit ratings demonstrated weaker debt-servicing 
capacity. The household debt-to-GDP ratio continued its long downward trend and stood below 
its historical median. The ability of households to service their debt remained solid, and most 
homeowners had significant equity in their properties. Delinquency rates on Federal Housing 
Administration (FHA) loans, typically associated with borrowers with lower credit scores and 
less equity, have been trending higher since 2022. Delinquency rates for auto loans remained 
well above their historical median (see Section 2, borrowing by businesses and Households).
3. Leverage in the financial sector. vulnerabilities associated with financial leverage remained 
notable. The banking sector remained sound and resilient overall. b anks continued to report 
regulatory capital levels near historically high levels, but fair value losses on fixed-rate assets 
remained sizable and sensitive to changes in long-term interest rates. Leverage at hedge 
funds reached the highest level since the data began in 2013 and was concentrated in a small 
number of large funds. This leverage supports a broad range of trading strategies, including 
those involving Treasury securities, interest rate derivatives, and equities. Leverage at the 
largest life insurers stayed at historically high levels. Leverage at broker-dealers continued 
to be low, in line with levels observed over the past decade, and their intermediation activity, 
including for Treasury securities, has increased in recent quarters (see Section 3, Leverage in 
the Financial Sector).

4. Funding risks. Funding risks have remained moderate. banks’ reliance on uninsured 
deposits—an important component of their funding risk—was well below the peaks in 
2022 in the aggregate, and fewer banks were as heavily reliant on such funding as they 
had been during that period. Redemption requests at certain perpetual-life nontraded 
business development companies (bDCs) have been trending lower more recently, though 
funds continued to limit the size of these redemptions and net flows remained negative. Life 
insurers’ nontraditional liabilities grew further in the second quarter of 2026. Finally, assets 
in cash-management vehicles continued to grow, largely in government money market funds 
(MMFs), which historically have been the least susceptible to large-scale investor redemptions 
(see Section 4, Funding Risks).

outreach (reported in the box “Survey of Salient Risks to Financial Stability ”). box 5.1 shows that 
when asked about risks to U.S. financial stability, a wide range of market contacts who partici -
pated in the Survey of Salient Risks in September and October most frequently cited risks from 
artificial intelligence (AI), risk asset correction, higher long-term rates, cyberattacks, and fiscal 
debt sustainability.

1 Asset Valuations
Asset valuations remained elevated, while term premiums increased
Asset valuations remained near the upper end of their historical ranges across most markets. 
broad equity valuation metrics continued to be elevated, partly driven by market enthusiasm for 
SI, though moderated by concerns over firms’ capital expenditures and debt issuance plans. 
Spreads on corporate bonds and loans remained narrow compared with historical levels. Since 
the previous report, Treasury term premiums rose notably and volatility increased on net. 
CRE prices were stable. In residential real estate markets, prices stayed substantially above their 
historical relationship with fundamentals, although nominal house price growth remained slow.
Table 1.1 shows the sizes of the asset markets discussed in this section. The two largest asset 
markets are those for publicly traded equities and residential real estate, which are substantially 
larger than the next two largest markets, Treasury securities and CRE. The table also shows 
recent and historical real growth rates for selected asset classes. The remainder of this section 
presents the status of vulnerabilities across these markets.
Treasury yields rose markedly, and volatility moved higher
Treasury yields across 2- and 10-year maturities climbed markedly since the May report and 
remained well above their average levels over the past 15 years (figure 1.1). A model-based 
estimate of the nominal Treasury term premium—a measure of the compensation that investors 
require to hold longer-term Treasury securities rather than shorter-term ones—rose notably above 
its historical median (figure 1.2). Interest rate volatility implied by interest rate swaptions moved 
higher in September and October, rising above its median level (figure 1.3).
Equity valuations remained high, and volatility subsided
Measures of equity valuations have remained elevated since the previous report despite 
increases in earnings expectations that have pushed down the forward price-to-earnings ratio 
over the past year. Nevertheless, that ratio remained well above its historical median, partly 
driven by market enthusiasm for SI (figure 1.4). The difference between the forward earnings-
to-price ratio and the real 10-year Treasury yield—a crude measure of the additional return that 
investors require for holding stocks relative to risk-free bonds (the equity premium)—remained 
near the lower end of the range of its historical distribution ( figure 1.5).2 Two measures of equity 
market volatility—option implied and realized—have decreased since May, with option-implied 
equity volatility moving to a level below its historical median ( figure 1.6).

Key financial markets functioned in an orderly manner
Market liquidity refers to the ease of buying and selling an asset. Low liquidity can amplify the 
volatility of asset prices and result in larger price moves in response to shocks. In turn, increased 
volatility can further reduce market liquidity because liquidity providers may become more cau -
tious in providing quotes. In extreme cases, low liquidity can threaten market functioning, leading 
to a situation in which participants are unable to trade at a reasonable cost.

Box 2.2. Leverage in the Data Center Buildout
Debt issuance to fi  nance advanced computing infrastructure has grown rapidly over the past year. The 
composition of the debt being issued is also evolving from mostly corporate bonds to a combination 
of public and private debt, structured credit, and project fi  nance.1 In addition, large technology fi  rms 
are rapidly increasing future lease commitments, backstop guarantees provided to other fi  rms, and 
other complex and contingent obligations.
Advanced computing–related debt issuance rose to more than $680 billion by October 2026, about 
a fi  vefold increase from $130 billion in all of 2023 (fi g u r e  A).2 Investment-grade technology compa-
nies issued most of this debt, and advanced computing–related borrowing as a share of nonfi  nan-
cial investment-grade corporate debt issuance increased from less than 6 percent in 2023 to about 
31 percent in 2026. Outstanding public debt related to advanced computing is estimated at more 
than $1.1 trillion as of October 2026. Sizable transactions also occur in the private credit market, 
making the exact magnitude of advanced computing–related debt outstanding more diffi  cult to 
measure.

Long-term contracts, leases, and guarantees are all ways in which hyperscalers and other large fi  rms 
with strong credit ratings can support the fi  nancing of projects developed by weaker-credit fi  rms 
without having an immediate, direct effect on the balance sheets of the stronger-credit fi  rms. For 
example, hyperscaler fi  rms often enter into long-term leases for data center capacity with third-party 
data center developers. These leases are then used by the third-party developers to secure fi  nancing 
for the data center’s construction. In addition, graphics processing unit (GPU) makers often provide 
a guaranteed residual value of the GPU collateral to support purchases of their GPUs by advanced 
computing technology or large language model (LLM) fi  rms. These debt fi  nancings linking hyperscalers 
and GPU designers with smaller fi  rms are often supplemented with equity investments by the hyper-
scalers and GPU designers, which create a “circular fi  nancing” dynamic.
The rapid growth of these other obligations represents deepening connections between the largest 
technology fi  rms and smaller, riskier fi  rms with less access to credit. via these connections, the larg-
est technology fi  rms may absorb losses in the event of a future downturn, mitigating their effects on 
the rest of the fi  nancial system. However, it also suggests that these fi  rms’ economic exposure to 
such a downturn may be substantially larger than suggested by their on-balance-sheet liabilities.
The hyperscaler and GPU fi  rms’ on-balance-sheet debt obligations are held primarily in mutual funds, 
pension funds, and insurance companies. A signifi  cant portion of these other obligations relate to 
fi  nancings that often involve special purpose entities (SPEs) that themselves issue both senior and 
subordinated debt. The riskier subordinated debt and equity tranches from these fi  nancings are held 
by high-yield bond funds, life insurance companies, bDCs, and other private debt vehicles. In addi-
tion, the largest U.S. bank holding companies have close to $1 trillion of exposure to large technology 
fi  rms, data center developers, advanced computing–related SPEs and private credit vehicles, and util-
ity companies, and those exposures are evaluated during the annual supervisory stress test.

4 Funding Risks
Vulnerabilities from funding risks were roughly in line with  
historical norms
Funding risks for most banks remained moderate. On the asset side, large banks continued to 
hold high-quality liquid assets (HQLA), with their liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) about unchanged 
at 120 percent of requirements. As a share of assets, uninsured deposits—a key component of 
funding risk for most banks—were stable at considerably lower levels than in 2022, both in the 
aggregate and among banks that had been heavily reliant on such funding.
Assets in cash-management vehicles remained at record levels, primarily reflecting assets in 
government MMFs, which have historically proved the least susceptible to large-scale investor 
redemptions among these vehicles. Runnable instruments as a share of GDP edged higher over 
the first half of 2026, and instruments susceptible to liquidity disruptions remained roughly 
stable as a share of total runnable instruments. The total market value of stablecoins stayed at 
around $310 billion over the past year.11
Life insurers’ nontraditional liabilities increased further over the first half of 2026. Redemption 
requests at some types of private credit vehicles have been trending lower, though they remained 
elevated. The ability of these vehicles to limit redemptions, together with their relatively low 
leverage, helped mitigate their exposure to run risk. Some open-end bond and loan mutual funds 
remained exposed to liquidity transformation risks; that is, they allow daily redemptions while 
holding assets that might become illiquid in times of stress.

Stablecoin assets plateaued
The market capitalization of stablecoins—digital assets designed to maintain a stable value 
relative to a national currency or another reference asset—remained relatively flat at around 
$310 billion over the past year amid lower crypto-asset prices. 14 The market continues to be  
concentrated among the two largest issuers ( figure 4.5).
The GENIUS Act (Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act), which 
will become effective on January 18, 2027, establishes a federal regulatory framework for the 
issuance and transaction of “payment stablecoins.” The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency 
has finalized its rules to implement the act, and other federal agencies have proposed rules to 
implement the legislation.
Bond and loan mutual funds navigated mixed market conditions 
without incident
Certain types of mutual funds, including those that hold corporate bonds or bank loans, can be 
particularly exposed to liquidity transformation risks, as they offer shareholders daily redemptions 
but hold assets that are less liquid and bear material credit and interest rate risks.

rate bond markets, holding approximately $1.7 trillion in U.S. corporate bonds, or 13 percent of 
U.S. corporate bonds outstanding (figure 4.6). Assets under management in mutual funds with 
holdings that are concentrated in bank loans and high-yield bonds—which are riskier and less 
liquid forms of debt—stood at $366 billion in August 2026, substantially below the high levels

bond and bank loan mutual funds through August 2026 (figure 4.8).

5 Near-Term Risks to the Financial 
System
The Federal Reserve routinely engages in discussions with domestic and international policy -
makers, community groups, and market participants to gauge the set of risk events that, should 
they occur, would be of greatest concern to these groups. As captured in the box “ Survey of 
Salient Risks to Financial Stability,” most of the respondents in recent outreach noted risks 
associated with new computing technologies, which include the possibility of a sharp correction 
in technology-related asset valuations, vulnerabilities stemming from complex financing arrange -
ments for data center investment, and the scope for cyber events enabled by frontier models 
that could have severe consequences. In addition, a larger percentage of respondents mentioned 
risks from high interest rates than had done so in the spring survey, while fewer participants cited 
risks related to private credit.
The remainder of this section describes three potential near-term risks and discusses how their 
consequences could interact with existing domestic vulnerabilities.
Disappointing returns on U.S. business investment could decelerate 
the economy and trigger declines in asset prices
With the development and adoption of new computing technologies advancing rapidly, private 
fixed investment has been a significant driver of U.S. macroeconomic growth this year. An unex -
pected downturn in corporate earnings could prompt declines in corporate asset valuations, a 
tightening of credit conditions, and a pullback from capital expenditures, reducing aggregate 
demand in the economy. A weaker economy could, in turn, stress businesses with high leverage 
and the most vulnerable households, leading to increased credit losses for banks and other 
lenders. Declines in corporate asset valuations could be amplified by sales of securities by lever -
aged and other risk-sensitive investors, potentially straining intermediaries that provide maturity 
and liquidity transformation.
Cyberattacks and other cyber events could disrupt market 
functioning and the provision of financial services
Survey respondents and other commentators have expressed recurring concerns about 
cyber-related risks to the financial system. Furthermore, recent advances in the ability of LLMs 
and agentic systems to detect and exploit vulnerabilities have introduced new challenges in 
safeguarding system security for financial institutions, infrastructures, and third-party service 
providers. Reports that agentic systems have been involved in cyber events have increased 
PAGE 56
48 Financial Stability Report48
in frequency since the May report, amid an overall increase in reported cyber events. Shocks 
caused by cyber events can be transmitted through indirect interdependence among financial 
institutions, market infrastructures, and service providers and can be further amplified by exist -
ing financial vulnerabilities. For example, a cyber event at a financial institution that impedes its 
ability to provide services could have wide-ranging effects, including degraded market liquidity, 
erosion of investor and depositor confidence, and forced sales of assets. Attacks on critical 
third-party service providers could affect multiple institutions, with the effects of such disruptions 
likely to be further amplified if substitutability for the affected services is limited. Through con -
tinued interagency coordination and information sharing, U.S. government agencies and financial 
regulators are advancing efforts to further protect the financial system and financial infra  structure 
from cyber risks.
Persistent geopolitical conflicts could affect U.S. financial stability 
through multiple channels
As discussed in previous reports, geopolitical tensions can lead to broad adverse spillovers, 
weighing on economic activity both in the U.S. and abroad, where elevated public debt levels 
limit some governments’ ability to respond to weaker growth.15 If geopolitical conflicts escalate, 
prolonged commodity shortages and impaired supply chains can put upward pressure on global 
inflation and lead to further increases in interest rates. A resulting increase in consumer borrow -
ing costs, along with inflation, could strain household budgets and weaken borrowers’ loan repay -
ment capacity. Higher interest rates also could lead to declines in the fair values of fixed-rate 
assets held by lenders, which, in turn, could reduce the supply of credit to the economy. Tighter 
financing conditions could also result from weaker investor sentiment, leading to reduced dollar 
credit from non-U.S. banks and sales of dollar debt securities by international investors that rely 
on less stable wholesale sources for dollar funding or for hedging exchange rate risk. 16

Box 5.1. Survey of Salient Risks to Financial Stability
As part of its market intelligence gathering, staff from the Federal Reserve bank of New York solic-

October, responses were received from 23 contacts, including professionals at broker-dealers, banks, 
investment funds, and advisory fi  rms. This box is a summary of the views provided by survey respon-
dents and should not be interpreted as representing the views of the Federal Reserve board or the 
Federal Reserve bank of New York.
AI was the top-cited risk, with responses noting concerns around elevated equity valuations, 
AI-enabled cyberattacks, and complex forms of AI-related fi  nancing (fi g u r e  A). Respondents also 
widely cited a correction in risk assets and a further rise in long-dated Treasury yields as key risks. 
Cyberattack and fi  scal debt sustainability risks were viewed as having the most severe potential con-
sequences. Across cited risks, mentions of AI, higher long-term interest rates, and cyberattacks saw 
the greatest increase relative to the spring survey ( fi g u r e  b).
Artificial intelligence
Respondents noted three distinct areas of concern: (1) elevated equity valuations and the possibility 
that lower-than-expected earnings could result in lower valuations and a market correction; (2) cyber-
security risk, stemming from either a malicious attack by sophisticated state actors or damage 
caused by frontier AI models themselves; and (3) risks associated with complex forms of AI-related 
fi  nancing.
Risk asset correction
Respondents widely cited the risk of a sharp correction in AI-related equities, driven by uncertain 
returns on large capital expenditures. Many noted that stretched valuations, circular fi  nancing 
arrangements, and heavy reliance on debt to fund the AI buildout could amplify losses, with the 
potential for a deterioration in broader risk assets to spill over to business investment and household 
consumption.
Higher long-term rates
A sustained rise in long-term Treasury yields was also widely cited, with respondents noting that 
drivers may include some combination of higher energy prices, deteriorating fi  scal dynamics, and 
reduced foreign demand for U.S. Treasury securities.
Cyberattacks
Cyberattacks on fi  nancial market infrastructure were seen as a growing risk. Many respondents noted 
that advances in AI are lowering the barrier to sophisticated attacks by state and non-state actors, 
while others pointed to the risk of unintended damage caused by frontier AI models themselves. 
Several respondents noted concerns that disruptions to trading, settlement, or payment systems 
could materially damage confi  dence in fi  nancial institutions.
Fiscal debt sustainability
Respondents cited a worsening of fi  scal dynamics in the U.S. as a key risk, with several noting con-
cerns that persistent defi  cits and heavy Treasury issuance could trigger a disorderly rise in yields.
